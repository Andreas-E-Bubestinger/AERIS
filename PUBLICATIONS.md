### Research Motivation

This work was motivated by a broader question in human–AI interaction: whether an AI system can derive sufficiently reliable information about a user's cognitive characteristics from natural interaction in order to improve the quality and appropriateness of subsequent interaction.

Potential applications include adapting aspects of:

- communication
- explanation depth
- information complexity
- reasoning support
- interaction style
- presentation of alternatives
- cognitive load
- collaborative problem solving

to the individual user.

The purpose of such profiling is not classification for its own sake.

The broader objective is to investigate whether an AI system can develop a sufficiently reliable and evidence-based understanding of relevant cognitive characteristics of the person it is interacting with, so that future interaction can become more appropriate, efficient, comprehensible, and individually useful.

Before such profiling can responsibly be used as an interaction mechanism, however, the underlying method must first be shown to produce meaningful results without being tuned to the target individual or contaminated by target-specific information.

The study therefore focuses on target blinding, leakage control, benchmark replication, and methodological prevalidation before attempting external calibration or stronger individual-level claims.

The objective is not to assign a definitive psychological or clinical label to a user.

It is to investigate whether corpus-derived cognitive profiling can eventually provide a sufficiently controlled and validated basis for adaptive human–AI interaction.

### Scope

The work presents a target-blind methodological framework for corpus-based cognitive profiling with particular emphasis on:

- leakage control
- benchmark replication
- methodological prevalidation
- reproducibility
- external calibration readiness
- separation between exploratory results and validated claims

The study is designed to evaluate whether corpus-derived cognitive profiling methods can be developed and tested without tuning the method toward the target individual or target corpus.

The publication represents methodological and prevalidation work.

It does not establish standardized individual cognitive scores, high-range psychometric equivalence, or externally calibrated predictive validity.

## Relationship to AERIS

AERIS and the research outputs listed here are related through a broader research programme concerned with:

- AI systems
- adaptive human–AI interaction
- methodological validation
- reproducibility
- provenance
- controlled inference
- governance of claims
- cross-model evaluation
- functional continuity
- user-adaptive system behaviour
- human and artificial intelligence interaction

Within this broader programme, cognitive profiling research is relevant to the question of whether an AI system can adapt interaction to an individual user on the basis of evidence derived from prior communication.

Such adaptation must remain distinguishable from unsupported assumptions, stereotypes, clinical diagnosis, or unvalidated psychological classification.

Not every publication associated with the researcher is necessarily a direct technical description of the AERIS architecture.

AERIS-specific implementation details may remain non-public even where related methodological research is published.

## Publication Principles

Public AERIS-related research follows several principles:

- distinguish observation from interpretation
- distinguish implementation from validation
- separate exploratory from confirmatory evidence
- preserve provenance
- document methodological limitations
- avoid claims beyond the available evidence
- retain reproducibility information where disclosure is appropriate
- distinguish user adaptation from unsupported user classification

Where a result remains unvalidated, provisional, architecture-level, or experimental, it is described accordingly.
